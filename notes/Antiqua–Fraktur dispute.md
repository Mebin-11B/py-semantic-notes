# [Antiqua–Fraktur dispute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiqua%E2%80%93Fraktur_dispute)

## Digest Summary
The Antiqua–Fraktur dispute was a German typographic controversy spanning the 19th and early‑20th centuries. Historically, Fraktur (black‑letter) was used for German texts while Antiqua (Roman) served Latin and foreign works, a convention that acquired ideological meaning after 1806: Antiqua was labeled “un‑German” and Fraktur as embodying German depth and sobriety. Nationalist figures such as Bismarck championed Fraktur; proponents argued it was more readable, compact, and culturally appropriate for Germanic languages. A 1911 Reichstag vote narrowly rejected a proposal to make Antiqua the official typeface. During the Nazi era, Fraktur was initially promoted as “German script,” but Hitler’s 1934 criticism led to a 1941 edict (signed by Martin Bormann) that banned black‑letter and Kurrent hand‑writing in favor of Normalschrift (Antiqua). After World War II, Latin scripts prevailed, though Fraktur persists in some signage, advertising, and among Amish/Mennonite communities. Small groups continue to advocate its cultural heritage, but its use is limited to niche contexts.

The Antiqua–Fraktur dispute was a [typographical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typography) dispute in 19th- and early 20th-century Germany.

In most European countries, [blackletter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackletter) [typefaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typeface) like the German [Fraktur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraktur) were displaced by the creation of the [Antiqua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiqua_(typeface_class)) typefaces in the 15th and 16th centuries. However, in Germany and Austria, the two styles of printing coexisted until the first half of the 20th century.

During that time, both styles gained [ideological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideology) connotations in Germany, which led to long and heated disputes on what was the "correct" typeface to use. The eventual outcome was that the Antiqua-style typefaces prevailed when the [Nazi Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_Party) chose to put an end to the use of Fraktur in favor of "normal typeface" ([German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_language): Normalschrifterlaß).

## Origin

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Antiqua%E2%80%93Fraktur_dispute&action=edit&section=1)]
Two typefaces: the German text uses Fraktur; numerals and Latin and French words are written in Antiqua (1768).

Historically, the dispute originates in the differing use of these two typefaces in most intellectual texts. Whereas Fraktur was preferred for works written in German, for [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) texts the Antiqua-style typefaces were normally used. This extended even to English–German dictionaries; the English words would all be written in Antiqua, and the German words in Fraktur. Originally this was simply a convention.

## 19th century

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Antiqua%E2%80%93Fraktur_dispute&action=edit&section=2)]

Conflict over the two typeface styles first came to a head after the occupation of Germany and dissolution of the [Holy Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Roman_Empire) by [Napoleon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon) in 1806, which led to a period in the [history of Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Germany) in which nationalists began to attempt to define cultural values common to all Germans. There was a massive effort to [canonize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_canon) the German national literature—for example, the [Grimm Brothers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grimm_Brothers)' collection of [fairy tales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fairy_tales)—and to create a unified German [grammar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammar).

In the context of these debates, the two styles became increasingly polarized: Antiqua typefaces were seen as "un-German", and using them took on connotations of "shallow", "light", and "not serious". In contrast, Fraktur, with its much darker and denser script, was viewed as representing allegedly German virtues such as depth and sobriety.

During the [Romantic Era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanticism), in which the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages) were glorified, Fraktur additionally gained the (historically incorrect) interpretation that it represented German [Gothicism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gothic_art). For instance, [Goethe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goethe)'s mother advised her son to refrain from using the un-German Antiqua “for God’s sake”.

[Otto von Bismarck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_von_Bismarck) was a keen supporter of German typefaces. He went so far as to refuse gifts of German books in Antiqua typefaces and returned them to sender with the statement Deutsche Bücher in lateinischen Buchstaben lese ich nicht! ('I don't read German books in Latin letters!').[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiqua%E2%80%93Fraktur_dispute#cite_note-FOOTNOTEReinecke191079-1)

## 20th century

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Antiqua%E2%80%93Fraktur_dispute&action=edit&section=3)]

The dispute between Antiqua and Fraktur continued well into the 20th century. Arguments for Fraktur were not only based on historical and cultural perceptions, but also on the claim that Fraktur was more suited for printing German and other Germanic languages, as their proponents claimed it to be more readable than Antiqua for this purpose.

A 1910 publication by [Adolf Reinecke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adolf_Reinecke?action=edit&redlink=1), Die deutsche Buchstabenschrift, claims the following advantages for using Fraktur as the German script:

- German script is a real reading script: it is more readable, i.e. the word images are clearer, than Latin script.[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiqua%E2%80%93Fraktur_dispute#cite_note-FOOTNOTEReinecke191042,_44-2)

- German script is more compact in printing, which is an advantage for fast recognition of word images while reading.

- German script is more suitable for expressing German language, as it is more adapted to the characteristics of the German language than the Latin script.

- German script does not cause [nearsightedness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myopia) and is healthier for the eyes than Latin script.[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiqua%E2%80%93Fraktur_dispute#cite_note-FOOTNOTEReinecke191042,_49-3)

- German script is still prone to development; Latin script is set in stone.

- German script can be read and understood all over the world, where it is actually often used as ornamental script.

- German script makes it easier for foreigners to understand the German language.[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiqua%E2%80%93Fraktur_dispute#cite_note-FOOTNOTEReinecke191058–59-4)

- Latin script will gradually lose its position as international script through the progress of the Anglo-Saxon world (here the author states that "Anglo-Saxons in the UK, the United States and Australia are still 'Germanic' enough to annihilate the Latin-scriptler's dream of a Latin 'world-script'").[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiqua%E2%80%93Fraktur_dispute#cite_note-FOOTNOTEReinecke191062-5)

- The use of Latin script for German language will promote its infestation with foreign words.

- German script does not impede at all the proliferation of German language and German culture in other countries.

On 4 May 1911, a peak in the dispute was reached during a vote in the [Reichstag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reichstag_(German_Empire)). The Verein für Altschrift ("Association for Antiqua") had submitted a proposition to make Antiqua the official typeface (Fraktur had been the official typeface since the foundation of the [German Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Empire)) and no longer teach [Kurrent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurrent) (blackletter [cursive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cursive)) in the schools. After a long and, in places, very emotional debate, the proposition was narrowly rejected 85–82.

### Nazi period

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Antiqua%E2%80%93Fraktur_dispute&action=edit&section=4)]

[Nazis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazis) had a complex and variable relationship with Fraktur. [Adolf Hitler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adolf_Hitler) personally disliked it. In fact, as early as 1934 he denounced its continued use in a speech to the [Reichstag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reichstag_(Weimar_Republic)):[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiqua%E2%80%93Fraktur_dispute#cite_note-6)

Your alleged Gothic internalization does not fit well in this age of steel and iron, glass and concrete, of womanly beauty and manly strength, of head raised high and intention defiant [...] In a hundred years, our language will be the European language. The nations of the east, the north and the west will, to communicate with us, learn our language. The prerequisite for this: The script called Gothic is replaced by the script we have called Latin so far [...]

Nonetheless, Fraktur typefaces were particularly heavily used during the early years of the [Nazi era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_era), when they were initially represented as true German script. In fact, the press was scolded for its frequent use of "Roman characters" under "Jewish influence", and German émigrés were urged to use only "German script".[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiqua%E2%80%93Fraktur_dispute#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMichaud2004208,_215–216-7) However, Hitler's distaste for Fraktur saw it officially discontinued in 1941 in a Schrifterlass ("edict on script") signed by [Martin Bormann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Bormann), which asserted that it was falsely called "Gothic" and actually consisted of [Schwabacher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schwabacher) Judenlettern ("Jewish letters").[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiqua%E2%80%93Fraktur_dispute#cite_note-Erlass-8)

One of the motivations seems to have been compatibility with other European languages. The edict mentions publications destined for foreign countries, Antiqua would be more legible to those living in the occupied areas; the impetus for a rapid change in policy probably came from [Joseph Goebbels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Goebbels) and his [Propaganda Ministry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reich_Ministry_of_Public_Enlightenment_and_Propaganda).[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiqua%E2%80%93Fraktur_dispute#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMichaud2004216–217-9) Readers outside German-speaking countries were largely unfamiliar with Fraktur typefaces. Foreign fonts and machinery could be used for the production of propaganda and other materials in local languages, but not so easily in German as long as the official preference for Fraktur remained.

Normalschrifterlass by Martin Bormann

Bormann's edict of 3 January 1941 at first forbade only the use of blackletter typefaces. A second memorandum banned the use of [Kurrent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurrent) handwriting, including [Sütterlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sütterlin), which had only been introduced in the 1920s. From the academic year 1941/42 onwards, only the so-called Normalschrift ("normal script"), which had hitherto been taught alongside Sütterlin under the name of "Latin script", was allowed to be used and taught. Kurrent did remain in use until 1945 for some applications such as cloth military insignia badges.

## After the Second World War

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Antiqua%E2%80%93Fraktur_dispute&action=edit&section=5)]

After the war, the [Sütterlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sütterlin) script was once again taught in the schools of some [states of Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/States_of_Germany) as an additional script, but it could not hold out against the usage of Latin [cursive scripts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cursive). As a consequence, most Germans find it difficult to decipher their own grandparents' letters, diaries, or certificates.

However, the Fraktur script remains present in everyday life in some pub signs, beer brands and other forms of advertisement, where it is used to convey a certain sense of rusticity and oldness (compare the English [ye olde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ye_olde)). However, many of these deviate from the traditional letterforms, specifically in the frequent untraditional use of the round s instead of the [long s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_s) (ſ) at the beginning of a syllable, the omission of [ligatures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typographical_ligature), and the use of letter-forms more similar to Antiqua for certain especially hard-to-read Fraktur letters such as k. Books wholly printed in Fraktur are nowadays read mostly for particular interests. Since many people have difficulty understanding blackletter, they may have trouble accessing older editions of classic works in German.

A few organizations such as the [Bund für deutsche Schrift und Sprache](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bund_für_deutsche_Schrift_und_Sprache?action=edit&redlink=1) [[de](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bund%20für%20deutsche%20Schrift%20und%20Sprache)] continue to advocate the use of Fraktur typefaces, highlighting their cultural and historical heritage and their advantages when used for printing [Germanic languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germanic_languages). But these organizations are small, somewhat sectarian, and not particularly well known in Germany.

In the United States, Mexico, and Central America, [Old Order Amish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amish), [Old Order Mennonite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Order_Mennonite), [Old Colony Mennonite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Colony_Mennonites), and [Hutterite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hutterite) schools still teach the [Kurrent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurrent) handwriting and [Fraktur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraktur) script. Many German books printed by Amish and Mennonite printers use the Fraktur script.
